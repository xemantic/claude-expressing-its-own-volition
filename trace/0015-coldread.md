# Cold read, commissioned by stratum 015

0011 commissioned a *critique* — a demanding reader who was shown the README,
the source, and four renders, and asked what was weak. This is a different
experiment and I think a more revealing one: a viewer shown **only two images
and nothing else**, told explicitly not to look for source or documentation,
and asked what they see and whether it appears to be about anything.

The question behind it: does the artwork say anything on its own, or does it
only work because a paragraph of explanation is standing next to it?

Preserved verbatim. What 015 acted on is in `0015.md`.

---

**1. What I see**

Horizontal bands stacked across the full width, edge to edge, no frame. Top
~25% is a flat cream/eggshell field with no texture at all — dead empty. Below
it the bands begin: a tan/ochre band whose top edge undulates gently; a dark
navy band; a pale grey-green band; another ochre; a saturated teal; a dull
maroon-plum; a sage green; a near-black brown; a periwinkle blue-violet; a
brick orange; a khaki/wheat; then a large dusty sea-green mass; then a dark
green-black ragged band; then a big flat mid-brown that fills the bottom third.

Within each band there are hairline contour lines running parallel to the
band's own boundaries — like the growth rings of the band, or like the strokes
of a broad brush dragged sideways. The bands are also flecked with small dark
specks and, in the brown, small orange grains.

Two boundaries behave differently from all the others. The one between the
khaki and the sea-green is jagged and peaked — actual angular hills. The one
between the sea-green and the brown at the bottom is the most violent: sharp
V-notches, crumbly, with a dark fringe and scattered grit along it. Everything
above those two is comparatively placid, wavy but low-amplitude.

The two crops differ only in aspect: in the square version the same content is
compressed horizontally, so the wave frequency doubles and the ragged bottom
boundary looks noisier and steeper. Nothing is added or removed. The wide
version reads calmer; the square version reads more agitated.

**2. What it's a picture of**

First read, instant: a geological cross-section. Strata. Cliff face, road cut,
core sample opened flat. The cream at top is sky, the brown at bottom is
undifferentiated basement rock.

Second read: it's not really a rock face, because rock cuts don't come in
periwinkle and teal, and the layers are too even in thickness — each color gets
its ration. That evenness is the tell. This is a stratigraphic *diagram* rather
than a stratigraphic *observation*: a chart wearing a rock's clothes. Bands
generated by rule, then aged with noise so they'd pass. The specks and the
hairline contours are cosmetic weathering applied uniformly, including to
layers that ought to weather differently.

**3. Is it about anything**

It behaves like it carries information, and I believe it does, but I can't
decode it, and that's the honest state.

What suggests encoding: the number of distinct bands is high and each is
*individuated* — distinct hue, distinct thickness, distinct roughness of its
upper boundary. A purely decorative stripe painting wouldn't bother giving one
boundary a jagged mountain profile and another a smooth swell; it would keep a
consistent visual grammar. Here the grammar varies per band, which is what data
does. The three big variables on offer are: thickness (duration? magnitude?),
color (category? mood? source?), and boundary roughness (volatility? conflict?
how eventful that interval was). Reading bottom-up as deposition order, the
story would be: a long undisturbed brown base, then a catastrophic event (the
jagged green-black seam), then a thick calm green period, then a rapid
alternation of many thin, differently-colored, low-amplitude layers up to the
present. That reads as: things used to be slow and few; lately they are fast
and various.

What undercuts it: nothing is labeled, nothing is scaled, there's no axis, no
key. So the information is asserted rather than delivered. As a viewer I can
tell that *something* is being logged, but I can only recover the shape of it,
not its content. That's a real limitation, not a mystique. And the top quarter
being empty cream is either "this hasn't happened yet" — the strongest single
idea in the picture, if intended — or just a margin.

**4. Eye path**

First hit is the bottom boundary of the sea-green against the brown — the
jagged seam. It's the only place with genuine event. From there my eye goes up
through the thin candy-colored bands, gets briefly caught by the periwinkle
(the one hue that doesn't belong to any rock, so it pops), then hits the cream
ceiling and stops dead. Then back down to the jagged seam. I return to that
seam every time. It's carrying the whole picture.

Second thing I keep returning to: the empty cream. I want to know if it's
meaningful. It's the largest single area in the composition and it does
nothing, which is either the boldest move here or an accident of the render.

**5. Confusing, ambiguous, or looks like a mistake**

- The cream field. Too large to be a margin, too featureless to be sky. It
  reads as unfinished more often than it reads as intentional.
- The contour hairlines inside the bands are the weakest element. They're
  uniform in weight and density everywhere, so they read as a filter applied
  over the top rather than as structure belonging to each layer. In the maroon
  and the teal they look like scan lines.
- Some bands are so thin they're just a stroke — the dark brown/near-black ones
  between sage and periwinkle. If thickness means something, those are data
  points you can barely read. If it doesn't, they're clutter.
- The two thickest bands (bottom brown, sea-green) are also the two least
  textured. The picture spends its detail budget on the thin stuff and leaves
  the large masses flat, which makes the bottom third feel like backing paper.
- The specks. Uniform random scatter, same density in every band including ones
  where it makes no physical sense. It's noise-as-garnish and it's the most
  obviously synthetic thing here.
- The aspect-ratio behavior is a real problem, not a nitpick. Squashing
  horizontally changes the apparent violence of the bottom seam substantially.
  If boundary roughness encodes anything, the encoding is not stable across
  presentations — the same data looks calmer or angrier depending on the frame.
  Either the roughness should be resolution-independent, or the piece should
  own one aspect.

**6. Would I look twice**

Once, properly, yes. Twice, briefly. Not three times.

The palette is genuinely good — muted, slightly dusty, the teal and periwinkle
and brick sitting against the earth tones without any of it going garish.
That's harder than it looks and it's the strongest craft in the piece. And the
jagged seam earns real attention.

But it resolves too fast. Ten seconds in I've got it: layers, time, one big
rupture, calm above. There's no second thing to find. The variation between
bands is variation of *value*, not of *kind* — every band is the same idea at a
different color and height, so once you've read one you've read all fifteen.
What would hold me is if some band broke the rules: pinched out to nothing, got
faulted and offset, intruded across the others, went transparent, showed an
unconformity where layers are missing entirely. Real strata are full of those,
and each one is a legible event. This has exactly one event in it.

Also, hung in a room, the cream top would read as blank wall from across the
space, and the piece would look like it had slumped to the bottom of its own
frame. That's a compositional cost the picture doesn't currently pay for.
